## How to Read This Document

On the afternoon of Saturday, October 17, 2026, a national protest day in France turned violent in a dozen cities. In Limoges — a quiet provincial city of about 130,000 people in west-central France — a group of masked people broke into the city hall, set it on fire, and watched as the 143-year-old building's bell tower collapsed into the flames. By the next morning, social media was full of claims about who had done it, and some of those claims pointed at Muslim and immigrant communities.

This document is an anatomy of that event. It is built the way an investigator builds a case file: primary sources first (police, prosecutors, fire services, court records, on-the-scene video), then official statements, then journalism, then social media claims — with every claim carrying a label saying where it comes from and how much weight it can bear.

One promise, made plainly and kept in the same spirit: "complete" in this document's title means *complete as of its cutoff — the third day, October 19, 2026, with everything located by then included and everything not located named as missing.* It does not mean the whole story, which is still being written by a court in Limoges, a Senate vote on October 27, and a reconstruction that will take years. Part 9 is the ledger of what this document does not know; the rest is what it does.

A few terms you will meet along the way, explained once:

- **A *casseur*** (literally "breaker") is the French word for a rioter who smashes, loots, or burns — used by officials and media alike. It carries no ethnic meaning.
- **"Ultragauche"** ("ultra-left") is the French government's standard label for militant far-left networks — black blocs, antifascist collectives — as distinct from ordinary left-wing parties. Officials used it heavily on October 17.
- A ***black bloc*** is not an organization but a tactic: a crowd of people dressed alike (usually black, hooded, masked) so that no individual can be identified, moving together inside a larger march.
- An ***attroupement armé*** ("armed gathering") is a legal term: a gathering where participants carry weapons capable of hurting people. It is one of the charges the Limoges prosecutor opened her investigation under.
- An ***émeute*** ("riot") is also a legal term with a precise definition, which matters enormously for insurance — we will come back to it.
- **OQTF** (*obligation de quitter le territoire français*) is a deportation order — an official command to leave France. You will see it once in this document, and the context will matter.
- **QPV** (*quartier prioritaire de la cité*) is France's official label for its roughly 1,500 poorest, state-targeted neighborhoods.
- A **préfecture** is the State's local headquarters in each department — the prefect's office, and a building protesters habitually approach with mortars.
- The **gendarmerie** is France's military-style national police, stationed in barracks across the country; the **CRS** (*compagnies républicaines de sécurité*) are the national police's riot units; the **IGPN** is the inspectorate that investigates police officers themselves.
- **LFI** (*La France Insoumise*) is Mélenchon's radical-left party; **LR** (*Les Républicains*) the traditional conservatives; **RN** (*Rassemblement national*) the far-right National Rally; **UDR** is Ciotti's small sovereignist party.
- A **flash-ball** is the police's "less-lethal" rubber-bullet launcher; its projectiles take eyes. A **Kalashnikov** needs no translation — which is the point of the quote you will read.

One more thing before we begin. This document corrects its own record openly. In the first hours after the fire, several widely repeated "facts" — including a dramatic inventory of weapons and a crowd-size estimate — turned out to be recycled descriptions of a different event from three months earlier. Where that happened, you will read about it, because the recycling is itself one of the most important findings of this investigation.

## PART 1 — The Day Itself: An Hour-by-Hour Account

### 1.1 The morning: a carnival of grievances

Saturday, October 17, 2026 was a national protest day — the convergence of several movements that had been building for weeks. High school students had been blocking schools for a month over teacher shortages, crumbling buildings, and university-application chaos. Former Yellow Vests — the leaderless movement that had shaken France in 2018–19 over fuel prices — had issued a joint appeal with the students under the slogan *"Jaunes et jeunes, nos luttes se rencontrent"* ("Yellows and young people, our struggles meet"). Unions, climate groups, and farmers' networks joined in. The stated theme was *vie chère* — "the high cost of living." The road to October 17 had run for a month: a single blockade at Créteil on September 21 had metastasized into a national school movement; climate marches put 131,000 people into 260 towns on September 26; firefighters struck September 27–29, public-service workers on the 29th; the students' national day on October 6 drew a claimed 450,000. A spring TikTok call to "block everything" had flopped; this time it stuck.

Fuel was the emotional core of the day. France had been through an energy shock since a war involving Iran began in late February 2026, driving oil — priced against Brent, the global benchmark — from a 2025 first-half average of around $72 a barrel to a second-quarter 2026 average of $103.8. The organizers' appeal site cited diesel up 86.6% since 2017, the minimum wage up only 26.1% in the same period, heating oil up 127%, household gas up 154%, electricity up 42%, the cost of living up 23.4%, even the baguette up 18.4% — and an austerity budget freezing benefits while energy companies posted record profits.

In Limoges, the day began calmly. There was a 10 a.m. gathering at the Valoine roundabout — a historic Yellow Vest spot from 2018–19 — and at 11 a.m. a "picnic protest" on Place Denis-Dussoubs organized by Thierry Durant, an ex-motorcyclists'-rights coordinator, who deliberately kept his event separate from both the Yellow Vests and the students. The main march was called for 2 p.m. at the Tourny crossroads.

**Who organized the Limoges march?** The honest answer: nobody, officially. This is a crucial fact. Across the entire region — the departments of Haute-Vienne and Corrèze — the only formally declared demonstration that day was a union rally in Tulle, an hour away. The powerful regional CGT union had publicly refused to join the day's actions ("Seule certique, la CGT 87 ne s'y mêlera pas"). The Limoges march existed only as social-media calls — some of its posters were even AI-generated. Veteran Yellow Vest figures (Éric Drouet, Jérôme Rodriguez, Priscilla Ludosky) had issued calls to mobilize; Jacline Mouraud, the movement's co-founder turned Reconquête spokesperson, refused to march and warned days beforehand: "Saturday is going to be extremely violent." As one senior police source told BFM two days earlier, describing the national picture: this time there were none of the small general assemblies that had preceded last year's blockades — "no assembly at all is organized. There are declared marches at the prefectures (the State's local headquarters), but we expect numerous undeclared actions." A national police assessment that morning anticipated around 250 actions nationwide and warned that the ultra-left would likely try to hijack afternoon marches. **This was a predicted event.** Everyone — police, ministers, even veteran protesters — saw October 17 coming. [Source: ICI Limousin, BFM, Le Populaire du Centre, France 3, La Dépêche]

The march that set off from Tourny at 2 p.m. was, by all eyewitness accounts, a cheerful, heterogeneous crowd — Yellow Vests, students, families, even a brass band. France 3's reporter described banners spanning teachers, health workers, and small business owners. The authoritative account arrived the next day, in the prefecture's own communiqué — the document that supersedes every newsroom's running estimate. At 2 p.m., the prefecture recorded **2,000 participants in the Casseaux sector, of whom more than 500 were "systematically seeking confrontation."** That number matters and will matter again: five hundred prepared aggressors is a very different fact from a spontaneous crowd, and it sits between the prefecture's early vague phrase ("several hundred... heterogeneous, explosive") and the mayor's much larger estimate (5,000 marchers, 600–700 black blocs — his figures, delivered in anger two days later, and recorded as his). The official count: 4 police vehicles destroyed, 4 officers seriously injured (31 injured in total across the weekend's files), and 19 people in custody within two days. For national scale: the Interior Ministry counted 550,000 demonstrators in 110 marches (110,000 in Paris); organizers claimed 800,000. [Source: préfecture de la Haute-Vienne communiqué, France 3, ICI, actu.fr]

### 1.2 4:30 p.m.: the highway

Around 4:30 p.m., a group the ICI reporter described as "mostly young people" split off from the march and occupied the A20 motorway — the main north–south artery through the region. Both directions were blocked between exits 28 and 36, trapping kilometers of traffic; the prefect then closed the highway *anticipatorily* — a deliberate protective decision, because motorists on that stretch "had previously been attacked, without reason," as the prefecture's communiqué notes. This specific location is a recurring flashpoint in Limoges: the Casseaux interchange, where the highway runs along the Vienne river, had already seen marchers try to storm the roadway during the 2023 pension-reform protests.

Two police vehicles were burned at the blockade. Firefighters intervened. The occupation lasted roughly an hour and a half. [Source: ICI Limousin, France 3, Le Populaire du Centre]

### 1.3 6 p.m.: the city hall

By around 6 p.m., hundreds of people had gathered on the parvis — the forecourt — of the Hôtel de Ville in the city center. Then the atmosphere broke.

According to multiple eyewitness accounts carried by ICI and AFP, the police on the square were overwhelmed and retreated inside the building. Two municipal police vehicles parked on the forecourt were overturned and set alight. Trees on the square caught fire. The local newspaper's account, the most precise we have, says **about fifty people forced the doors of the city hall around 6 p.m.** The report of Agence France-Presse — the national news agency whose dispatches most of the world's press was running that evening — describes what happened next: *"After burning a municipal police car outside the town hall, masked individuals broke windows to get inside and set a fire."*

Within about half an hour, the ground floor was ablaze. The prefecture's communiqué, published the following day, states the attacker's intent in the starkest official language available: at 6 p.m. the rioters forced their way in and set the fire **"while staff and members of the public were still inside the building."** Inside, by the mayor's account, the intruders told the gatekeepers — municipal employees earning about €1,500 a month — "We're going to gut you," and told elected officials, "We're going to kill you all." Those words are the mayor's, delivered at a press conference; the legal system would soon adopt their logic. Firefighters arrived shortly after 6 p.m. — and here is a detail that tells you something about the crowd: the firefighters were, in ICI's words, "copiously applauded by the demonstrators, who cleared a passage for them through the crowd." The people who cheered the fire crews and the people who burned the building were standing in the same square. [Source: Le Populaire du Centre, AFP via Europe 1/France 24/Yahoo, ICI]

The fire behaved the way fire behaves in a 19th-century building: it found the roof. By 7:40 p.m. the blaze had largely consumed the roof structure; the right-hand gable was completely destroyed; firefighters worked to stop it reaching the central body of the building. At around 8:30 p.m., with video cameras rolling, **the 43-meter bell tower — the campanile, the symbol of the city — collapsed into the flames.** At 8 p.m., the prefect of Haute-Vienne, Maurice Barate, told AFP the building was "largely lost," adding: "The roof has collapsed onto the lower floors, except the west wing, and two-thirds of the exterior has already disappeared." He called it "a catastrophe for heritage" and "a deliberate act by hooligans." Around 8:30 p.m., CRS riot police — the national force's dedicated crowd-control units — cleared the square with tear gas; by 9 p.m. relative calm had returned. [Source: France 3 Nouvelle-Aquitaine, AFP via CNEWS/France 24/Yahoo, ICI, BFM]

### 1.4 What burned inside

The damage was not only architectural. The wedding hall and the civil-registry offices — the rooms where Limoges residents married and registered births — were gutted. Les Échos' live desk added that the **municipal council chamber itself** was among the rooms hit. A municipal employee was photographed in tears in the arms of an elected official. [Source: France 3, 21news, Les Échos, AFP]

What was *not* destroyed matters too, for accuracy's sake. Early headlines — including from local outlets — said the building was "completely destroyed." France 3's on-site reporting was more precise: firefighters saved the central part of the building, and the prefect noted the west wing survived. The municipal archives raise a subtler question: current working registers were on the office floors, but bound historical registers are held in municipal archive vaults and digitized copies exist at the departmental archives — so claims of "irreversible loss" of Limoges' civic memory were journalistic inference, not established fact. France 3 put the damage in the **tens of millions of euros**. [Source: France 3, préfecture via AFP, archives.haute-vienne.fr]

### 1.5 The same evening, elsewhere

For context, Limoges was the extreme point of a national day of violence, not an isolated incident. Two official numbers frame the scale problem that every argument since has fought over: the Interior Ministry's own eve-of-day forecast was 40,000–60,000 in Paris and roughly 100,000 elsewhere — against 110,000 and 550,000–800,000 who actually came; and the national deployment was about 10,000 officers, one-eighth of the force mustered for September's blockade day, with reinforcements recalled mid-afternoon. The day had opened with its own warning: around midday, roughly a hundred masked people attacked the Paris headquarters of CNEWS and Europe 1 with projectiles (21 arrests) — the media themselves a target of the day's first register — while in the provinces a speed camera was torched painted with "17/10" and a National Rally office was vandalized. The same hours saw: the Marseille city hall's grand hall briefly invaded (with chants of *"C'est la Révolution, on prend la Bastille"* — "It's the Revolution, we're taking the Bastille"); Marseille's mayor, Benoît Payan, said of the intruders: "I want them in prison."; the Lille city hall doors forced; the Calais and Angers city halls targeted; the Bordeaux prefect's residence sacked (the RAID elite unit deployed); a Lyon tourist office burned and shops looted; a Marseille police station attacked with Molotov cocktails; Paris seeing its République square pillaged, cars burned "to the applause of part of the crowd," running battles 400 meters from the Élysée Palace — the presidency itself — a gendarmerie barracks attacked while officers' families were inside; the Paris headquarters of CNEWS and Europe 1 rained with projectiles by about a hundred masked people (21 arrests) — the media as the day's first target. The day's fire nodes reached well beyond Limoges: Lyon's tourist office; the Lille McDonald's on the Grand-Place and a bar beside it; a children's leisure structure in Valenciennes; a university building in Rouen burning in the night; and, in Saint-Étienne, the neighborhood town-hall annex set alight at 4:35 p.m., twenty minutes after the grenade tore off the teenager's hand. By evening, Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez reported roughly 400 arrests nationwide, the "vast majority" adults. The consolidated figures kept climbing after he spoke: **2,397 arrests** and **997 injured police, gendarmes, and firefighters, seven of them seriously**, by the following evening. On the other side of the ledger, 111 protesters were reported injured. One person died in Paris — a cardiac arrest on the Boulevard de Magenta, carried by bystanders to the Château-d'Eau fire station, dead before care could begin; the identity was not published, and nothing in the record connects the death to police action or to violence. In Saint-Étienne, a 17-year-old protester lost a hand after picking up a police grenade — an encirclement grenade, by the account of witnesses who blamed the minister's decision to re-authorize such weapons two days earlier; both police inspectorates are seized. In Limoges itself, 4 officers were seriously injured. [Source: RFI, franceinfo, AFP, Instagram/AFP, Wikipedia, préfecture de la Haute-Vienne]

And one political act that day deserves recording because of what came later: in the Paris march, masked people hanged an effigy of Marine Le Pen. The far-right later cited this as proof of the day's political character. [Source: CNEWS live blog]

## PART 2 — The Building: Why This Fire Hit a Nerve

To understand the reaction — the weeping elected officials, the president's statement, the sense of a threshold crossed — you need to know what burned.

The Limoges Hôtel de Ville is not just an office block. Its architect, Charles-Alfred Leclerc, won the Grand Prix de Rome and worked on the Palace of Versailles before designing it. Financed by the 1875 bequest of a wealthy local, Alfred Fournier, it was built between 1876 and 1883 and inaugurated on July 14, 1883 — deliberately, on the national holiday. Its architecture openly quotes the Paris Hôtel de Ville, which Leclerc had competed to rebuild after the Commune. Its façade — fifty-one meters wide, in the restrained Louis XIII style — carries two allegorical statues by the sculptor Tony Noël: *L'Orfèvrerie* and *L'Émaillerie*, Goldsmithing and Enamelwork, the twin crafts that had made Limoges rich for five centuries. Four ceramic medallions by Giandomenico Facchina honor the city's famous sons — the Renaissance enamelist Léonard Limosin, the chancellor d'Aguesseau, the revolutionary Vergniaud, General Jourdan. Inside hung two vast oil paintings marouflé onto the walls by Henri-Paul Motte. The 43-meter campanile held a clock faced with the city's coat of arms, visible across the rooftops; at its foot, in the square renamed for Jacques Chirac in 2019, stood the Genuys fountain of 1892. Every one of these named things — statues, medallions, paintings, clock, tower — was inside the fire's reach. The building has been listed as a historic monument since 1975, which means no improvised rebuild: an architect of the Bâtiments de France will supervise whatever comes next, and the doctrine will be reconstruction as it was. In 2015 — tellingly, for what comes later — the city and the Fondation du Patrimoine had already launched a public patronage appeal for its renovation: even before the fire, the monument was asking for help to survive.

In other words: a building whose entire purpose was to embody the city and the Republic — raised in the decade when France was rebuilding the very symbol of its republican self-government — was torched by people chanting on its forecourt. That is why a mayor cried on television, why the Socialist president of the departmental council — the elected assembly of the département, the administrative tier between the region and the city — called it "an attack on the symbol of the Republic," and why the prime minister convened a crisis cell.

The State's heritage arm moved within a day. Rachida Pégard, the culture minister, called the attack on public buildings and heritage "unspeakable," confirmed the building "heavily hit," and announced that the regional heritage directorate (DRAC) and state services were mobilized on Sunday with "emergency works without delay." The prefecture's own communiqué settled the vocabulary: the town hall was "largely destroyed" — the roof gone, the right gable gone, the wedding hall and civil-registry floors gutted, the council chamber burned, and by Sunday morning, as a local radio journalist walked the perimeter, "only the structure still stands; the visible timber frame, consumed." A fire-service drone had filmed the blaze from above and captured the moment the bell tower fell — the collapse video that circled the world is, in its original form, official aerial footage. [Source: Le Monde, préfecture de la Haute-Vienne, France 3, ICI]

## PART 3 — Who Did It: What Is Actually Known

### 3.1 The knowns

**The method.** The assault followed a pattern visible across France that day and well documented in previous unrest: police vehicles burned first (on the highway, then on the forecourt), then public buildings, then shops. Masking (cagoules) to defeat identification. Mortar fireworks — the signature projectile of French urban unrest since 2023 — fired at police on the highway and near the prefecture. Roughly fifty people forced the doors; masked individuals entered and ignited the fire. [Source: Le Populaire, AFP, ICI, France 3]

**The legal case.** The prosecutor of Limoges, **Émilie Abrantes**, opened her investigation on Saturday night for "participation in an armed gathering and violence against police officers." By Sunday the case had been re-qualified — and the re-qualification is the most important judicial fact in the file. The town-hall fire is now investigated as **"destruction of property by dangerous means endangering persons, committed by an organized gang"** — a charge that carries up to twenty years' criminal imprisonment in its aggravated forms, and one that reaches beyond the hands that held the match to the structure around them. The file has been assigned to the **DCOS**, the national police's organized-and-specialized crime division, with the forensic unit dispatched from Toulouse; investigators are interviewing the people who were inside the building. The supporting statute is blunt: ten years for fire set by means dangerous to persons, and the organized-gang aggravation doubles the ceiling — because, as the code frames it, what matters is the danger to people, and the prefecture's communiqué confirms there were people.

A second file runs beside it, and the two must not be blurred. The first arrests — 19 people in custody by Sunday evening — concern the *pillaging*: 8 looted shops, 3 stolen cars, and the street violence that injured 31 officers, 4 of them seriously. The prosecutor's published profile of the 17 held on Sunday morning reads: 13 men and 4 women, 7 minors, the youngest 14, the oldest 25, and 13 of the 17 with no criminal record at all. That is a youth file — local, amateur, mostly clean — and it is *not* the town-hall file. The arson file, as of the last check, had produced no published profile and no named suspect. [Source: procureure's communiqués via Ouest-France, TF1, ICI, BFM; Code pénal art. 322-6 via legal analyses]

**The prosecutor matters.** Abrantes, appointed in July 2024 and installed that October after six years in Brive-la-Gaillarde, had run this exact playbook before. After the July 2025 riots in Limoges' poorest neighborhood (of which more below), her office opened an investigation under nearly identical charges. Her track record includes a major museum burglary solved through CCTV exploitation — relevant, because Limoges had just received a **€200,000 state grant for video-surveillance cameras** during an April 2026 visit by Jean-Didier Berger — now the minister carrying the casseurs-payeurs bill — and the city has a police supervision center. [Source: JORF, Le Populaire, France Bleu, Ville de Limoges bulletin]

### 3.2 The known unknowns

**Who were the fifty?** As of this document's cutoff, no identified suspect, no age profile, no address profile, no group affiliation has been published for the city-hall assault. What exists is inference, and honest inference looks like this:

- *They were a small, prepared core inside a large crowd.* The forecourt held hundreds; the door-breakers were dozens. The prefecture's own phrase — "heterogeneous, explosive procession" — describes a march that fragmented, not an invasion.
- *Nationally, the day's arrested were adults and outsiders.* In Paris, of 195 arrests by mid-afternoon, only 13 were minors and only one arrestee resided in Paris. The Marseille prefect, Jacques Witkowski, described "7,000 to 8,000 rioters... mainly young adults." The Interior Ministry's own model of the day was two waves: organized ultra-left groups in the afternoon, then "opportunist delinquents" attaching themselves. Small traveling groups, moving fast, deliberately dispersing police. [Source: Radio France/parquet de Paris, La Provence via Nuñez, TF1/LCI, Le Monde]
- *"Ultra-left" is a real organizational layer, not just an insult.* France's interior ministry has been naming it since 2023 ("a very clear ultra-left handprint," Nuñez said), and the anatomy is documented: groups like Rennes' Défense Collective (whose dissolution was annulled by the courts in February 2026 — just as the courts had earlier blocked dissolving the Soulèvements de la Terre environmental movement, showing the legal ceiling on proscribing movements) provide legal training before demonstrations, bloc tactics during, and legal support after; the Jeune Garde Antifasciste was dissolved in June 2026 after the February killing of far-right militant Quentin Deranque in Lyon — seven people, most tied to the group, were indicted, two of them parliamentary staffers of the group's founder-deputy, Raphaël Arnault. A scholar of anarchism, Olivier Cahn, adds the necessary caution: a black bloc is a tactic, not a group, and those arrested at them tend to be "the least experienced" — including, classically, children of the bourgeoisie. [Source: BFM, Observatoire des violences de rue, Marianne, LCP]
- *The foreign element, documented.* On the Monday after the fire, the flagship evening news of TF1 reported from the national casseur files something no official had said aloud: among the black blocs were people who "are not only French — some came from Italy, Switzerland, or Germany." That is the first named, mainstream attribution of a foreign presence in the violent layer — and its nature matters: European ultra-left travelers moving along their own circuits, not immigrants. The same reportage described the method as choreography: the organized groups "launch the confrontation, then withdraw, leaving room for the looters." Police seizures that weekend included improvised weapons and **fake police armbands** — the gear of people who plan to be mistaken for the forces they attack.

*An expert hypothesis, labeled as one.* The sociologist Thomas Sauvadet, asked on a regional live blog who the violent were, answered with deliberate caution: two profiles are *possible* — "young people from the far left, increasingly violent and experienced in urban guerrilla warfare," and "young thugs from the priority neighborhoods accustomed to street combat with the police," citing the 2023 riots and the 2025–26 victory celebrations as the second group's prior battlefields. A hypothesis, both profiles, no evidence attached to Limoges — recorded here because it is the closest any named expert has come to the origin question, and because honesty requires showing where informed speculation ends and evidence begins.

*A preview of the trials.* France has run this pipeline before, and the template is on record. In June 2026, after victory-night riots in Paris, 225 adults were taken into custody; about fifteen faced immediate trial the following Monday in three courtrooms, a fourth opened Tuesday. Among them: a 20-year-old Algerian living and working in Spain, sentenced with a five-year ban from French territory — the one foreign-national case in that sample, real but rare; a young man acquitted outright because a policeman's contradictory statements left reasonable doubt; and a teenager caught with ten mortars, two smoke grenades, a taser and two knives "intended for resale" — his explanation: "a stupid way to make ends meet." Expect the same architecture — speed, heavy demands, identification fragility — when the October files reach the courts.

*But no official source attributes the Limoges arson to any organization, ideology, or community.* Prefect Barate: "hooligans." Abrantes: an armed gathering. The mayor: "delinquents" who "will be arrested." The Interior Minister: ultra-left groups plus opportunist delinquents. The Prime Minister: ultra-left plus looters. Nobody — at any level, in any channel — has offered an ethnic, religious, or origin-based characterization of the perpetrators. When French authorities do need physical descriptions operationally (as in an October 2025 child-abduction alert), they publish them; nothing of the kind exists here. [Source: AFP, communiqués, TF1/LCI, X statements]

### 3.3 The local escalation that everyone in Limoges already knew

This is the part of the story the international coverage missed entirely. The October 17 fire did not come out of nowhere. It was the fifth node of a fifteen-month escalation chain, each step documented:

1. **July 18–19, 2025 — the Val de l'Aurence riots.** In Limoges' poorest neighborhood, a night of guerrilla-style violence: a burning stolen car lured police into an ambush; between 70 and 150 masked people armed with mortars, Molotov cocktails, iron bars, and bats attacked them for three hours — some of the projectiles thrown from apartment windows above. Barricades went up on the RN141, the road toward Angoulême, where the ambushers filtered traffic: some drivers' cars were stolen, others burned. In the ugliest scene, an off-duty officer's car was surrounded by the mob — with his children, aged seven and eleven, inside. Nine to ten officers were injured, most with burst eardrums, one with a hand injury. Police fired 241 defensive munitions (flash-balls, tear gas, stun grenades, clearance rounds); gendarmerie squads had to extract their municipal colleagues. The riot was the climax of a week that had begun around July 14 with copycat violence across the region; elite CRS units were trucked in for the following weekend. Eight residents filed complaints; the city's deputy police director called them the most serious events in years; the mayor called it "organized urban guerrilla warfare," insisting — presciently, and controversially — that there was "no pretext": "nothing... just the desire to destroy and to show that the territory belongs to you." [Source: ICI, Le Figaro, franceinfo, AFP]
2. **Late July–August 2025 — arrests with revealing roles.** Four minors (14, 15, 15, 17) were arrested. Their admissions map the division of labor inside such groups: one admitted firing a mortar and degrading the bait car (a car-jacking); another admitted his role was *filming scenes to post on social media*. The children's judge imposed, on the younger of the two, a ban on entering the Val de l'Aurence and a 22h–6h curfew unless accompanied by a legal representative; the older one was placed under provisional educational supervision until their hearing — set for October 15, 2025, in the Limoges children's court, where the two July arrestees joined the two August arrestees before the same bench. [Source: ICI]
3. **October 1, 2026 — the first city-hall contact.** During student protests, the city hall's door was damaged and the square degraded. Mayor Guillaume Guérin filed a criminal complaint on October 2. [Source: Ville de Limoges, actu.fr, Le Populaire]
4. **October 2, 2026 — mortars in the streets.** Police arrested twelve minors for degradations, projectile-throwing, **mortar fire**, and violence against police; firefighters treated a 19-year-old with an eye injury. [Source: France 3]
5. **October 13, 2026 — rehearsal.** About 1,000 students marched; some 200 youths attempted to blockade two city-center locations, were dispersed with tear gas, and later **confronted police at the Hôtel de Ville itself**. [Source: France 3]
6. **October 17, 2026 — the fire.** [Everything in Part 1.] Within two days the judicial machine had produced 19 custody cases — for the pillaging and street violence, with a published profile (minors as young as 14, most without records) that belongs to that file and not to the arson investigation, which continues separately and has named no one.

Each node was smaller than the last escalation; the fire was the largest. And each node was handled — or not handled — by the same small group of named local officials: prosecutor Abrantes, prefect Barate, Mayor Guérin (since March 2026), his predecessor Émile Roger Lombertie, and the police leadership who had publicly begged for reinforcements in July 2025 ("we have crossed a threshold... we need at least 50 more officers daily," said Laurent Nadeau of the Alliance police union). [Source: ICI]

## PART 4 — The Claim: "Muslims Did This"

Here is the question this document was built to examine, stated plainly: **what is the evidence linking the arson to Muslim immigrants or communities?**

The answer, after tracing every available thread, is: **there is no such evidence, and no official or journalistic source has offered any.** But the story of how the claim lives, where it comes from, and why it is structurally likely to spread anyway — that is a story worth telling in full, because it reveals the machinery of modern disinformation better than any textbook.

### 4.1 What the evidence shows instead

**The official record is origin-neutral at every level.** Prefect: "hooligans." Prosecutor: "armed gathering." Interior Minister: "ultra-left movement plus opportunist delinquents" (his exact model: an organized militant core providing cover, then delinquents grafting on). Prime Minister: "ultra-left groups organized the violence; delinquents joined in to pillage and destroy." Mayor: "delinquents." Not one word, anywhere, about origin, religion, or ethnicity. [Source: AFP, LCI/TF1, X statements, ICI]

**The geography argues against the import of the banlieue template.** France's two great waves of urban riot — 2005 and 2023 — followed a documented banlieue logic: concentrated in Parisian suburbs and poor peripheries, strongly correlated with priority neighborhoods, immigrant populations, and social housing; 2005 touched 200 communes, 2023 over 550 — pillage in 209 of them, and 87% of the communes touched had priority neighborhoods. October 17, 2026 was a *different genre*: a national, declared-march day (110 marches, up to 800,000 people) whose violent fringe applied the 2023 "razzia" repertoire (retail looting, public buildings, police targeting) inside central city cores — the geography of the 2018–19 Yellow Vests, not of the 2005 banlieues. Limoges' city hall stands in the bourgeois center of a mid-sized city, far from any priority neighborhood. Mapping the day's events onto the 2005/2023 demographic template is a category error the data itself contradicts — before any origin question is even asked. [Source: research by Guillaume Le Gall's team at Sciences Po's AFRICA programme for Le Monde; Senate report l24-695]

**The demographic background, stated carefully.** None of this is evidence about October 17 — it is the honest state of knowledge about the terrain. In Limoges itself, the metropolitan area counts about 24,700 residents in its nine official priority neighborhoods — 11.9% of the population — and the Val de l'Aurence, the site of the July 2025 ambush, is among the twenty poorest neighborhoods in France: median incomes around €6,400–7,500 a month per household, a poverty rate near 62%, about a third of adults foreign nationals. Nationally, INSEE has long measured immigrants at roughly 28% of priority-neighborhood populations — two and a half times their share in cities generally. On offending patterns, the best-documented foreign dataset is Danish: non-Western male descendants are convicted at a little over three times the rate of the rest of the population, a ratio that falls to about 2.5 after age adjustment and narrows further — without disappearing — after socioeconomic adjustment, with researchers flagging ethnic profiling and differential police attention as residual biases. France itself publishes no ethnic crime statistics; official silence on origin is the rule, which is precisely why unsourced origin claims about October 17 carry no weight either way.

**The organizers' own text points the other way.** The mobilization's appeal site (17octobregiletjaune.net, archived in full during this investigation) is a class-and-price grievance document: diesel prices, minimum wages, frozen benefits, energy-company profits. Its approximately one thousand individual signatories are visibly multiethnic — many French-Muslim and North-African names among them — and its text explicitly invokes the October 17, 1961 massacre of Algerian demonstrators by Paris police ("Sixty-five years later, racism still serves to divide those who have the same ends of the month"). Whatever one thinks of the movement, its own self-presentation is anti-racist, and no religious content appears anywhere in the appeal. Its tactical coda — "Film, post, share, tell" — is a media doctrine, not a call to arms; the same division of labor (one throws, one films) had already been documented among the arrested minors of July 2025. The date itself was triple-loaded: besides the 1961 anniversary, October 17 is the World Day for the Refusal of Poverty — and, as the TikToker who launched the date ("v2.95," whose September 8 video passed three million views) explained, it was simply *"a Saturday, during the holidays, everyone can be there."* [Source: archived site, La Croix, Beur FM, Elysée 1961 commemorations]

**Adjacent facts that exist — recorded precisely because precision is the antidote to splicing.** There are real, documented items in the ecosystem that a disinformation producer could stitch into a false narrative, and honesty requires listing them:

- A rap song recorded by high schoolers in the Loiret referencing the terrorists Mohamed Merah and Salah Abdeslam triggered a formal investigation for apology of terrorism — genuine Islamist-terror-referencing content, inside part of the youth movement. [Source: Yahoo/AFP]
- The most prominent influencer arrested for incitement before the big day, "Rayan PSN" (806,000 TikTok followers; lives in Dubai), told his audience he wanted "two or three riot chiefs... guys with OQTF [deportation orders] who want to wreck everything." Paris's prefect, Patrice Faure, had referred him to prosecutors five days earlier — his live had also drawn a Pharos report, the French platform for flagging illegal online content, for apology of terrorism — and police carried an active file on him. He was arrested on the night of October 17–18. His use of migration-status language was an incitement to *others*; it is not evidence about who burned anything, and he has no known connection to Limoges. [Source: RMC, BFM, Le Parisien]
- At a July 2025 march in Limoges protesting the former mayor's remarks (below), a protester named Fatiha Zemani — who describes herself as from one of the oldest Muslim families of Limoges — warned that such rhetoric could provoke "civil war." Her warning was about *words*, not riots. [Source: RMC/BFMTV]

None of these facts touches the identity of the fifty people who entered the city hall. But they are exactly the kind of raw material the disinformation pipeline feeds on.

### 4.2 Where the claim actually comes from

**Seed one: political rhetoric, offered without evidence.** On October 17 itself, two national politicians moved the frame from "ultra-left" toward identity. Eric Ciotti (UDR, mayor of Nice) posted that "Islamo-leftism threatens the Republic... the massive presence of Palestinian flags, of communal slogans... movements close to the Muslim Brotherhood" — in "these demonstrations," nationwide, with no evidence attached. Jordan Bardella (RN) spoke of "a convergence of ultra-left terrorism and delinquent populations from the banlieues." Neither said anything about Limoges; neither offered documentation; the "Palestinian flags" element appears to conflate the day's protests with Marseille's long-standing *weekly* Saturday pro-Palestine rally, which coincided in the same city-space. No indexed footage verifies "massive" Palestinian-flag presence inside the *vie chère* marches; none disproves sporadic individual presence either. The claim, as stated, is unverified. [Source: X statements via La Dépêche live; assopalestine13 weekly-rally records]

**Seed two: the American amplifier pipeline.** Since late September, a documented machine has been recycling French unrest into a "France is falling to mass migration" narrative: former US president Donald Trump ("This isn't about schools, this is about Islam wanting to take over a once great Country!" — October 6; Foreign Minister Jean-Noël Barrot answered that no link between the student movement and any religious demand had been established); his son ("war zone"); US conspiracy broadcaster Alex Jones pushing "anti-white civil war" compilations built partly on a 2022 school-bus fire; British activist Tommy Robinson reposting 2022 footage as new. A NewsGuard investigation found one viral "assault on the Paris Stock Exchange" video was in fact April 2023 pension-reform footage, reposted by Africa-based accounts posing as American users (one impersonating Mel Gibson); the exchange confirmed no protesters had come near its building. Jones's compilations even claimed "at least a dozen Catholic priests set on fire" — investigators found no evidence that any priest was attacked. In the European Parliament's orbit, a German AfD parliamentarian called the unrest "severe race riots" — again, without data; Fox News ran "civil war" framing; the European disinformation monitor EDMO had documented the ethnic-blame pattern as early as October 8; and Hindu-nationalist accounts in India recycled it as well. A fact-check by France 24 documented the method: genuine clips, false dates, zero substantiation. The Limoges fire footage — dramatic, emotional, visually unambiguous — proved to be exactly the raw material this pipeline was waiting for. On October 18, the day after the fire, **Vice President J.D. Vance posted video of the burning Limoges city hall** with this message: "Western progressives imported the third world into France... neglected the fundamental questions of assimilation and compatibility. They were told this was how it would end and they did not listen. I pray that France... puts an end to this madness before more monuments burn. I hope Americans are paying attention. This is the future congressional Democrats want for our country." **Former president Trump wrote on his own platform**: "France is burning! Immigration of people from a totally different world, people who carry hate in their hearts, has broken this once-great country. Close your borders and take back control, before it is too late." The president of El Salvador added a security-export variant: France, he said, was "finished" — unless it applied his method. The claim's chosen imagery was Limoges: the fire of a provincial city hall became, within a day, stock footage in an American immigration argument — while the regional newspaper of that same city reported, plainly, "the vice president of the United States reacts to the video and blames immigration." [Source: France 24 Truth or Fake, AFP, Politico, NewsGuard, TF1 VÉRIF; 20 Minutes/Yahoo (AFP), Newsweek, Le Parisien, La République du Centre]

**Seed three: recycled local footage.** Here the investigation caught the machinery red-handed, three times in five days around Limoges alone. On October 17, CNEWS's Facebook feed recirculated a "new night of riots in Limoges" package — describing "a hundred masked armed individuals attacking vehicles" — that was actually **three-month-old footage from July 2025**. Then, on October 18, France 3's regional Facebook account repeated a dramatic police-union inventory of weapons ("between 100 and 150 masked individuals armed with Molotov cocktails, mortars, stones, iron bars, baseball bats") inside its October 17 coverage — an inventory we traced verbatim to a July 19, 2025 AFP interview with the same police-union official about the Val de l'Aurence riots. Even the local paper's Facebook feed briefly resurfaced the prosecutor's *October 2* communiqué about twelve arrested minors, undated. Early versions of this very document repeated those errors; they are corrected here deliberately, because the corrections demonstrate the core finding: **in the October information space, July and October keep bleeding into each other, and every recycled detail is a potential false fact.** [Source: CNEWS FB, France 3 NA FB, Brut/AFP July 19, Le Figaro, Le Populaire]

**Seed four: fabrication economics.** An AFP Factuel investigation named a TikTok account, "Gurumédia," that manufactures fake shocking quotes by ministers and opposition figures ("we will shoot again... even more amputations") to farm engagement — the same account-operator economics (platform creator payments; comparable operators earn €1,500–4,500/month) that will reward whoever produces the most inflammatory Limoges content, ethnic or otherwise. And France's foreign-disinformation watchdog VIGINUM has documented something worse: a foreign-run fake ecosystem (the "Rokh Solis" campaign) that built *fake Muslim-radical websites* and fake Catholic-sovereignist counter-sites to polarize France's March 2026 municipal elections — meaning some of the "Muslim" political content in France's 2026 information space is manufactured by foreign actors impersonating Muslims. Any future "Muslim claim" about Limoges must be screened against exactly this playbook. [Source: AFP Factuel, VIGINUM, cyberveille analysis]

**And the counter-current, equally documented.** Locally, the frame war predates the fire. In July 2025, then-mayor Émile Roger Lombertie (LR) described the Val de l'Aurence rioters using ethology — the science of animal behavior — spoke of absent fathers "in Muslim environments," and said the rioters had "the desire to destroy and to show that the territory belongs to you," declaring the neighborhood a "no-go zone." The sitting LFI deputy, Damien Maudet, filed a formal criminal referral — "the mayor assimilates the inhabitants of working-class neighborhoods of immigrant origin, or practicing the Muslim religion, to animals, to 'the behaviors of beasts,'" he wrote — alleging racist statements; the local Moroccan association marched silently through the city — a hundred to two hundred people, by organizer and police counts, filing past under banners to protest the words, not the police — at the call of its president, Hassan El Kanneb, who also heads the association of Moroccans living abroad and who had once successfully campaigned for direct air links between Limoges and Morocco ("The mayor has really broken us," he said). A protester named Fatiha called the remarks "of an extreme violence"; Atika: "We are residents and citizens of Limoges"; Amin: "counter-productive"; a majority councilor from his own camp, Vincent Léonie, called the stigmatization of Muslims "irresponsible," while the local Socialist leader, Thierry Miguel, said the mayor had "completely drifted." In June 2026, the new mayor Guérin courted the Muslim community at an Eid celebration ("to make a child, you need a father and a mother") — feminist and LGBTQIA+ groups attacked him from the left for the same sentence — and was attacked for "Islamo-rightism" by the RN and by the far-right professional provocateur Damien Rieu In other words: in Limoges, *both* ethnic framing of disorder *and* accusations of pandering to Muslims are established political weapons, wielded for years before anyone touched a match. The post-fire claim-space was pre-structured; the fire merely poured fuel into channels that had been dug long before. [Source: ICI, CNews, Boulevard Voltaire, Le Populaire, France 3, El Kanneb/AMRE statements]

### 4.3 The verdict on the claim

**Established fact:** masked individuals, in a crowd of hundreds, broke into the city hall and set it on fire; the investigation is open; no one has been publicly identified, arrested, or characterized by origin in connection with the arson.

**Established pattern:** French authorities' working theory — an organized ultra-left militant layer providing cover, plus opportunist, mobile, largely adult delinquents — fits the day's national evidence (arrest demographics, ministry modeling, documented organizational infrastructure) but remains **unproven for Limoges specifically** until identifications are published.

**Claim assessment:** the proposition that Muslim immigrants or Muslim communities were responsible for the Limoges arson has, as of this document's cutoff: zero direct evidence; zero official attribution at any level of the French state; zero named journalistic source for the Limoges case; no verified social-media origin traceable through indexed channels. What has changed since the first hours is the claim's *altitude*, not its evidence: it now sits at American head-of-state level, carried by the fire's own imagery — and it stands in direct, quotable opposition to the record. Agence France-Presse's wires, carried worldwide, state the rebuttal as standard background: "French authorities have not linked immigrants to the rioting, attributing some of the violence to radical far-left groups and others who joined demonstrations to loot shops." Two more layers deserve equal precision. First, the *documented* foreign element in the weekend's violence is of a wholly different kind: European ultra-left travelers, named by TF1 as coming from Italy, Switzerland, and Germany — a political circuit, not a migration one. Second, the question of foreign nationals has a real, evidence-based legal address that has nothing to do with the claim's insinuations: when a foreign national is convicted in a casseur file, the criminal sentence can found a refusal of residency or a deportation order — the mechanism used in June, when a Spanish-resident Algerian convicted after the victory-night riots received a five-year ban from French territory. That is how the state actually handles the intersection of crime and migration status: case by case, by conviction, on the record.

It should also be said what this investigation found on the fabrication side, because the claim lives in two different worlds at once. France's foreign-disinformation watchdog, VIGINUM, has attributed with high confidence to an Israeli-based non-state operation — a shadow entity called "Blackcore," with Swedish-registered front companies and a Tel Aviv address — a campaign that built *fake* Muslim-radical and fake Catholic-sovereignist websites to polarize France's March 2026 municipal elections. Fabricated "Muslim" political infrastructure, run from abroad, is a documented 2026 phenomenon — which is precisely why every authentic-looking "Muslim claim" about Limoges must be screened against that playbook before it is believed. Notably, VIGINUM's own assessment of October 17's *origin* was the opposite of the later claims: foreign amplification of the mobilization's beginnings was "minimal at that stage." One final sourcing note, in the interest of full hygiene: the criminologist Xavier Raufer, whose analysis of the crisis appeared in a widely read opinion piece, is a genuine academic whose long-standing "islamo-gangster" framing has been academically critiqued as belonging to the far right's construction of an "internal enemy" — record his pedigree wherever his voice is used.

The claim, in sum: an unverified narrative in search of facts — now spoken from the highest political platforms on earth, contradicted by every official channel in the country it concerns, fueled by machinery that is half organic and half fabricated, and waiting, like everything else in this file, on the one institution that can actually answer it: the investigation, whose first files were only days old when this document closed.

## PART 5 — The Patterns: What History Says About Days Like This

Individual events are accidents; patterns are structure. Here is the structure this investigation found, each pattern documented and each one explaining *why* October 17 happened the way it did.

**The escalation ladder.** Police vehicles first, then public buildings, then shops. Limoges: highway police cars (4:30 p.m.) → forecourt municipal vehicles → city hall → shops (BHV looted; FNAC attacked but preserved). The same ladder nationwide: Marseille's police station before its city hall; Lyon's clashes before its tourist office. This ladder has precedents going back through the 2023 riots (when 2,508 public buildings were burned or damaged in one week — among them 105 mairies — 12,202 vehicles were torched, 808 police were injured, and, at L'Haÿ-les-Roses, Mayor Vincent Jeanbrun's home was rammed with a burning car while his wife and two young children were inside) to 2005. October 17's novelty was the *top* of the ladder: never before had a mairie been burned to its bell tower during a national social march. [Source: Senate report r23-521, AFP, Le Monde]

**The armament grey market.** Where do urban rioters get military-grade fireworks? From a documented cross-border grey market: mortar barrages resold through Telegram, Snapchat, and TikTok for €40–50, imported mainly from Poland and Germany where sale is legal, bought by teenagers as young as 16. After the 2023 riots France banned their sale and transport — "it has become easier to buy a Kalashnikov than a firework mortar," as the national hunters' federation president put it; prefectures now issue emergency bans before protest days (the Eure on October 2, Seine-et-Marne on October 12); police arrest resellers (one on September 30, caught selling during student demos). The day's seizures elsewhere told the same story of preparation: two hooded men arrested on the Champs-Élysées carrying knife blades, and drill-mounted spikes seized in Paris — the improvisation of a milieu that plans. Limoges had already heard mortars on October 2 — two weeks before the fire. [Source: franceinfo, CNEWS, Police Nationale]

**The maiming-and-weapon cycle.** Every wave of French unrest now produces its signature maimings — eyes (flashballs), hands (grenades) — and every maiming produces a weapon-policy oscillation. This cycle ran its full course in just ten days: Lens, October 5 — a 15-year-old's hand torn off by a sound grenade; October 7 — the Interior Minister suspends sound grenades for the school movement; October 16 — he re-authorizes all intermediate weapons, including sound grenades, for the big day; October 17 — a 17-year-old in Saint-Étienne loses a hand picking up a grenade, and a Limoges-adjacent pattern had already appeared on October 2 (a 19-year-old's eye injury). In Tours, a police officer was indicted for shooting a 16-year-old in the eye. Each side's outrage feeds the other's mobilization. [Source: France 3, franceinfo, BFM, Le Figaro]

**The two-population model.** The school movement's arrests were overwhelmingly minors — 87% of over 5,000 taken into custody in early October, part of a machine that had processed 6,629 arrests and left 761 police injured by the Interior Minister's own October 7 count — more arrests than the entire eighteen months of the Yellow Vest era. October 17's were the mirror image: "the vast majority adults" (Interior Minister), 13 minors among 195 Paris arrests, "mainly young adults" in Marseille. Two different worlds: school-age protesters and traveling adult rioters, the second grafting onto marches the first fills. Any serious account of October 17 must keep them separate. [Source: justice ministry, parquet de Paris, TF1/LCI, La Provence]

**The vacuum march.** The day's most important structural feature was negative: nobody stewarded the marches. No general assemblies (unlike 2025), no service d'ordre, no declared Limoges organizer, the regional CGT abstaining. Police predicted exactly this — and predicted the hijacking. A stewardless march is an open chassis: a prepared hundred can steer where thousands walk. [Source: BFM police source, ICI]

**The mobile swarm.** The Interior Minister's evening diagnosis: rioters "split into very mobile groups to carry out simultaneous attacks and stretch police forces." Paris's arrest data shows the mobility: of 195 arrested, one lived in Paris. The 2018–19 Yellow Vests made highways and roundabouts their theater; their successors made *movement itself* the weapon. [Source: Le Monde, Radio France]

**The recycled footage engine.** Documented four times in five days around Limoges alone: CNEWS recycling July riot footage as breaking news; France 3's regional account recycling July's weapon inventory in October's frame; the local paper's Facebook feed resurfacing an undated October 2 communiqué; the fire service's own feed recirculating a September rail-fire item. Add the foreign pipeline (old Nîmes bus footage, 2022 Paris clips) and the fake-quote factory, and you have a complete picture of how the "facts" about Limoges were manufactured faster than the fire could be extinguished. The rule for readers: **date-check every clip before believing it** — the July and October events are visually interchangeable at low resolution. [Source: CNEWS FB, France 3 NA FB, Brut/AFP, SDIS 87, France 24, NewsGuard]

**The anniversary anchor.** October 17 is the date of the 1961 Paris massacre of Algerian demonstrators — a wound in French memory that the far right and the anti-racist left contest to this day. The organizers embraced it explicitly; the counter-memory machine ("France under occupation," "Papon was right") needs no invitation. October 17, 1961 was the night Paris police, acting under a prefect who would later be convicted of crimes against humanity for it, killed dozens — by credible counts more than a hundred — of Algerian demonstrators and dumped many of their bodies in the Seine; France took until 2021 to acknowledge the State's responsibility. Commemorations ran concurrently that same afternoon in Paris and its suburbs — while, hours later, a city hall burned in the provinces. The date guarantees that every October 17 event in France will be fought over through the lens of Algeria and Islam *regardless of who actually did what* — which is precisely why evidentiary discipline matters more, not less, on this date. [Source: appeal site, Beur FM, Elysée commemorations, EDMO]

**The under-forecast pattern.** The intelligence service predicted 40,000–60,000 in Paris and about 100,000 nationally; the day delivered at least 110,000 and 550,000–800,000. The deployment was scaled to the paper — 10,000 officers against 80,000 for a September day that drew far smaller crowds — and reinforcements were recalled mid-afternoon, in front of everyone. Whether one reads that as intelligence failure, budget logic, or the simple unreadability of a leaderless movement, the measurable gap between the forecast and the day became every side's evidence: the government called the violence unprecedented in organization; its critics called the preparation unprecedented in inadequacy. Both point at the same numbers.

**The two populations, visually confirmed.** The choreography the ministry described in words — organized groups launching confrontation, then withdrawing "to leave room for the looters" — was documented on camera: a flagship newscast's reporters watched small organized units disable security cameras before smashing into businesses, and police seizures included fake police armbands. The consultant Guillaume Farde drew the working distinction used by investigators: an organized ultra-left core with an ideology, and a second, later wave of "individuals whose goal is enrichment through looting — luxury, video games, sportswear, easy resale."

**The official script.** "Known individuals, ultra-left, video surveillance will identify them" — Interior Minister Darmanin, March 23, 2023. "We will do everything to find the authors... a very clear ultra-left handprint" — Interior Minister Nuñez, October 17, 2026. Same words, three years apart. The frame is institutional habit; its truth value for Limoges awaits the identifications. [Source: TF1, Le Monde]

**The local chain.** Part 3's five-node escalation (July 2025 → October 1 → October 2 → October 13 → October 17) is itself the deepest pattern: a small city's slow-motion slide, each node documented, each warning issued ("we have crossed a threshold," July 2025), each reinforcement arriving after the next escalation. [Source: ICI, Le Figaro, France 3]

## PART 6 — Follow the Money: Who Pays for a Burning City Hall

Fires have a balance sheet. Here is the complete one for October 17 — the losses, the payers, the laws that decide, and the interests swirling around it all.

### 6.1 The loss ledger

- **Limoges city hall:** "probably in the tens of millions of euros" (France 3) — a 143-year-old listed monument with a collapsed bell tower, gutted wedding hall, destroyed civil-registry offices and council chamber.
- **Four police vehicles** burned (municipal and national).
- **Shops:** the BHV looted, FNAC attacked (preserved) in Limoges; nationwide, the pillage hit precisely youth-consumer retail — Monoprix, H&M, Decathlon, Apple stores, McDonald's franchises, sports shops, jewelers — plus banks and tobacco shops (the 2023 pattern repeated: then, 200+ shops, 300 bank branches, 250 tobacco stores).
- **Historical benchmarks:** the 2018–19 Yellow Vests cost insurers €256 million; the 2023 riots left €793 million in final insured damage — three times the 2005 and Yellow-Vest costs, with professional property carrying 65% of the cost and local authorities 27% (the employers' federation put the total economic loss at €1 billion), with 2,508 public buildings damaged; New Caledonia 2024 came to some €900 million of insured loss inside €1–2 billion of total damage.
- **The hidden costs:** 80,000 police mobilized for September's blockade day alone; the CRS, RAID, water cannon, and the IGPN's investigations — the police force that investigates the police; five fire engines and a night of Limoges firefighters' labor; the justice system now preparing mass fast-track trials.

**The commerce track.** Within two days the retail federations had counted roughly **1,500 shops touched nationwide**, with a bill the federation president put at "several hundred million euros." Bordeaux's chamber of commerce alone counted some fifty businesses "seriously affected." The commerce minister and the prime minister jointly told merchants, "We are mobilized to help you," and the mayors of Paris, Lyon, and Marseille wrote to Matignon in one voice: the extent of the damage had to be measured, a shared initial assessment demanded — they were received the same day. For a small shop owner, the practical machinery matters more than the politics: declare within two working days (five for the contract's general deadline), photograph everything before cleaning, keep the debris for the expert, contest every invented "wear-and-tear" deduction — and if the insurer refuses, remember that French law makes *the insurer* prove the event was a riot before it can exclude coverage. An unverified estimate of €110–120 million in repair costs circulated on social media; nobody official had confirmed any figure as of this writing. [Source: FCD/LCI, CCI Bordeaux, Le Figaro, Le Parisien, legal analyses]

### 6.2 Who pays — France's riot-insurance machine

France has spent decades building the legal machine that assigns riot losses, and October 17 arrives at a peculiar hinge moment in its history.

**The current rule (still in force on October 17):** when a crowd causes damage, the **State is civilly liable** for "gatherings" damage unless it proves fault — a rule that put taxpayers on the hook for the 2023 riots and produced, in an extraordinary 2025 case, a New Caledonia court ordering the State to pay €28 million to an insurer — one clothing brand's single claim, handled by Allianz, was €736,000 for failing to anticipate and respond to the 2024 riots. But in July 2026, an appellate court overturned all fourteen of those judgments — fault confirmed in principle, recovery denied — dramatically narrowing the victim-sues-the-State route. For scale, the Senate's own tally of the 2023 riots' economic damage: two billion euros to retail distribution, €850 million to hotels and restaurants, €600–800 million to transport. [Source: Code de la sécurité intérieure L.211-10; TA Nouméa December 2025; CAA Paris July 2026 via L'Argus]

**The new law (not yet in force):** the February 2026 finance law created a **mandatory riot guarantee** in every property contract, backed by an industry mutualization fund and a public commission that must formally "qualify" an event as an émeute — legally defined as a collective action, with violence, directed against public authority, expressing protest or political/social demands. Tellingly, the Senate's definition was written to *exclude* "commando actions": the roughly fifty people who torched the Limoges city hall would likely fail the "mass" test as a standalone act. But the regime awaits Brussels' clearance under the EU's state-aid rules (which police government support to industries) and implementing decrees — **it does not apply to October 17 losses.** The industry's own design proposal — a solidarity levy of roughly 0.8% on all damage policies, floated by Groupama chief executive Thierry Martel — shows where the socialization of riot risk is heading, with the public reinsurer (the institution that insures the insurers) standing behind the scheme: the CCR, directed by Édouard Vieillefond, under insurers' association president Florence Lustman. [Source: Loi n°2026-103, art. 171; Senate report l24-695; Gide/Lexbase analyses]

**The practical consequence for Limoges:** the city hall's recovery runs through its existing property policy (old-generation contracts often excluded or capped riot risks — the very gap that inspired the new law), with a likely "wear-and-tear" gap on a 19th-century building (insurers deduct an age-depreciation share from payouts — on a 143-year-old monument, that deduction is large). The precedent is documented to the euro: after the March 2023 arson that burned the portal of Bordeaux's Palais Rohan, reconstruction cost €803,822, the municipal insurer paid €628,000 (78%), the city bore the remaining 22%, thirteen companies worked 3,500 hours for thirty months, and the restored doors were inaugurated in December 2025 — nearly three years after the fire. Limoges' loss is an entire building of that era, not a doorway. A humbler precedent sketches the funding stack to come: Garges-lès-Gonesse rebuilt its town hall in about two years after 2023 with State, regional and departmental money — and French policy doctrine ("new value for an identical building") favors reconstructing the monument as it was, not replacing it with something cheaper. The municipal-insurance market it will claim against is concentrated — two carriers hold more than forty percent of the local-authority market, one mutualist group (SMACL, based in Niort) has won over 800 public contracts worth €154 million since 2020 — and it is traumatized: 2026 had already produced €1.2 billion in storm claims and €500 million in wildfire claims before the riots began; after New Caledonia, local-authority premiums rose 25% in 2024, and some mairies have had insurers cancel their policies outright, as happened to Coulaines (Sarthe), whose rebuilt town-hall reception area cost €180,000 — €150,000 paid by the insurer, the rest by the State — and whose insurer had cancelled the policy before the rebuild was even finished — the under-insurance trap is systemic, not hypothetical. possibly supplemented by a dedicated state fund for local-authority equipment extended to riot damage, and by the disaster-playbook the government rehearsed in August after the Saumos megafire: a prefecture-managed solidarity fund (€12 million there, plus €5 million for farmers and €2 million for tourism, inside a relief package the Prime Minister said could exceed €100 million), fast-track permits, public naming-and-shaming of slow insurers, full business tax relief, and a "Notre-Dame-style" emergency legal article. The cathedral template itself carried a price tag of €860 million, funded by three distinct streams — €600 million from five wealthy families, €200 million from state-owned companies, €60 million from a global patronage program — the insurance-state-charity mix any monument rebuild now requires.

Within two days of the fire, that machine began to turn — under pressure. Standing before the ruins on Sunday, Mayor Guérin did something mayors of Limoges do not do: he threatened to resign, "with my whole council," within twenty-four to forty-eight hours unless the government produced a written plan. "I want a special plan for Limoges. I want to be given the capacity to rebuild this town hall," he said, invoking Notre-Dame. "It is not for the people of Limoges to pay for the damage" — he demanded "a rain of millions." The reply came the same day, in a letter from Prime Minister Lecornu: "The town hall of Limoges will be rebuilt, because it is the symbol of a nation that lets itself be neither beaten nor intimidated." The State, the letter said, would make "a direct budget contribution" to the reconstruction — the first explicit commitment of state money — alongside accelerated procedures, the mobilization of insurers "so that compensation arrives as quickly as possible," and a promise of transparency on "the responsibilities of the authors, their accomplices, and the role of ultra-left militants." A citizenship minister attended the Sunday march; the prime minister announced he would come to Limoges within the week, with several of his ministers, "to set a calendar, a method, and the means to be mobilized." The culture minister had already put the heritage services on site with emergency works. The first numbers anyone said aloud, meanwhile, circulated unverified — a social-media estimate of €110–120 million in repair costs, unattributed and uncorroborated; the mayor's promised precise figure had not been published as of this writing. [Source: Autorité de la concurrence, Weka experts, Bordeaux municipal accounts, franceinfo]

**The offender-pays turn:** parallel to the insurance reform runs the "casseurs-payeurs" bill — officially "relating to the repair of damage resulting from violence or degradations committed in public spaces and the recovery of damages" — championed personally by Prime Minister Lecornu since May's Champions League riots, with government spokesperson Maud Bregeon — who simultaneously runs the energy-aid portfolio — as its public voice, to be presented to the Senate on **October 27, 2026**. It answers a documented failure: the 2019 "anti-breaker" recourse against identified rioters has been almost never used, because the people caught rarely have money. The new law's answer is to widen the pool: collective responsibility of crowd participants (after a formal warning), per-person recovery up to €10,000, an exception allowing seizure of housing benefit and family allowances (normally untouchable, to guarantee a minimum to live on), recourse against parents of minors (Justice Minister Darmanin had already instructed prosecutors to use parental civil liability — 90% of the school-movement detainees were minors), and a double-edged clause letting the State sue a *municipality* if the municipality itself was at fault. The last clause matters uncomfortably for Limoges: the same city that just received €200,000 for cameras must now show its own security arrangements for the Hôtel de Ville were adequate. It answers a documented failure: the 2019 recourse against identified rioters has been almost never used, because the people caught rarely have money — the new law's answer is to widen the pool. In the Senate, the run-up is now named and dated: senators David Margueritte and Olivier Bitz, rapporteurs of a mission on the future of public-order policing, will present their conclusions on Wednesday, October 21, six days before the vote. And in the lower house, the speaker proposed a parliamentary commission of inquiry — with a deliberately double mandate: to examine "the methods of violent groups" *and* the policing strategy and its "possible malfunctions." The speaker's own February refusal of an inquiry commission into a different affair was immediately thrown back at her; the far right, for its part, rejects this one in the name of judicial speed. Every institutional party, in other words, is choosing which half of the day it wants investigated. [Source: franceinfo, Public Sénat, L'Humanité, BFM, Banque des Territoires; Sénat, Assemblée nationale]

### 6.3 The grievance economy — why fuel is the core of the story

Follow the money behind the anger and you find the war's profit center. Since the Iran war began in late February 2026, oil — priced against Brent, the benchmark barrel against which the world's crude is measured — averaged over $100 in the second quarter. TotalEnergies — chief executive Patrick Pouyanné — posted a first-half 2026 net profit of **$11.2 billion, up 72%** — the top contributor to a CAC 40 — France's forty biggest listed companies — whose members earned a record €73 billion in six months, up 34%. The same fiscal logic was written into the 2027 budget presented on October 1: benefits, housing aid and public-sector wages frozen, €5.5 billion found on retirees' pensions. Industry Minister Roland Lescure's own numbers admit 3 million people were eligible for fuel aid and only 1.2 million applied. The Prime Minister's October 7 response — releasing 10 million barrels of strategic reserves, expected to cut diesel 12–18 cents in November — came with the revealing confession: "During the last energy crisis, more than €70 billion was spent on shielding and aid. We cannot reopen those expenses." TotalEnergies, for its part, capped pump prices during the conflict at a cost its critics calculate at about 4% of one quarter's profit, paid a €1,000 advance to its French employees (rejected by the CGT as "gross manipulation" amid a refinery strike the union had called across all TotalEnergies sites for October 8 — the escalation of a pressure campaign that had already seen fishermen blockade France's largest fuel-storage site at Fos-sur-Mer in mid-September), announced €1.5 billion in buybacks, and raised its dividend 5.9%. The left costs its nationalization proposal at €70 billion; NGOs demand a superprofits tax. One man wears the day's two hats better than anyone: the president of the FCD retail federation who condemned the pillaging of his members' shops is also a senior LFI official. This is the economic engine under the mobilization — and everyone at the top of it knows the exact figures. [Source: TotalEnergies results, Le Monde, franceinfo, BFM, Maire-info]

### 6.4 The organizer economy and the outrage economy

The mobilization itself runs on almost no money: union dues (the CGT's contribution is 1% of members' net salary, worth roughly €75 million a year to its confederation, indirectly recycled through a 66% member tax credit and state dialogue funds), volunteer collectives with traceable genealogy (the "Bloquons tout" network descends from a Telegram loop called "Les Essentiels" run by Julien Marissiaux, and from Maxime Mayoral's "Indignons-nous" collective), a crowd-loan instrument introduced for the 2026 municipal campaigns, and a website with no disclosed budget. The parties circling the movement run on capped private donations (€4,600 per election to a candidate; €7,500 per year to a party; 66% tax credit) plus public money that follows votes — in 2025 the National Rally drew €14.8 million, the presidential majority €11.3 million, the Socialists €7.9 million. What the movement chose to *target*, by contrast, was economically literate: its published action map listed some 1,500 locations nationwide — 249 gas stations, 36 fuel depots, 5 refineries, 68 toll plazas, 53 train stations, 132 roundabouts — and its declared October 17 actions included the Esso-ExxonMobil refinery at Port-Jérôme. The investigators found **no evidence of foreign funding** — while France's disinformation watchdog has documented foreign money's actual role in 2026: not funding marches, but *fabricating the information space* around them.

The real money is in outrage. The incitement influencer monetizes attention (at his scale, platform payouts and sponsorships are worth thousands of euros monthly; his arrest threatens the whole revenue stream). The conspiracy broadcaster monetizes civil-war compilations. The professional provocateur monetizes ethnic framing. A fake-quote TikTok account farms engagement toward platform creator payments. A media group recirculates three-month-old riot footage because tension is its business model. And French law now stands ready, on October 27, to bill the fifty people in the masks — if, and only if, the cameras that the State paid for in April can tell us who they were. [Source: AFP Factuel, VIGINUM, market data, L'Obs, Le Figaro]

## PART 7 — The Response: Everyone's Move, Named

**The mayor, Guillaume Guérin (LR).** Six months into the job after a March election won in a fragmented first round (27.34% for him, 24.86% for the LFI candidate, 16.92% for the Socialists, 12.54% for the National Rally, 10.05% for the outgoing mayor, who then withdrew) and a three-way runoff he took 51.25% to 40.82% against the united left, the RN on 7.92%, at 62% turnout — nearly double the previous municipal election's. His campaign had warned that a left victory would turn Limoges into "an open-air ZAD" — his shorthand for the militant protest communes France has repeatedly dismantled by force, and one of his own list members, Monique Boulestin, had accused the united left of being "openly for the dealers and the delinquents." He stood outside his burning city that evening with colleagues in tears: "There are no words to condemn what happened... A monument is burning, but not the Republic. Criminal proceedings will follow. A message to the delinquents: you will be arrested." The city will file a criminal complaint; a precise damage assessment was commissioned; continuity of services promised. A march of elected officials was announced for Sunday. His posture — co-governing with Socialist mayors inside his metropolitan majority while talking law-and-order — is the tightrope he now walks, especially since his own political family has been attacking him from the right for "Islamo-rightism" since June. [Source: ICI, France 3, X statement, Le Populaire]

**The prefect, Maurice Barate.** "Largely lost." "A catastrophe for heritage." "A deliberate act by hooligans." And, hours earlier, his services' estimate of the march: several hundred people, "heterogeneous, explosive." The prefecture's coordination with the prosecutor's office and the €200,000 camera grant now become central. [Source: AFP via CNEWS/France 24]

**The prosecutor, Émilie Abrantes.** Enquiry opened Sunday morning: armed gathering, violence against police. Her office has run this file before — the July 2025 ambush case (four minors arrested; roles documented from mortar-firer to social-media filmer) and the October 2 school case (twelve minors, mortars) — and her museum-burglary case shows her appetite for video exploitation. Her stated priorities on arrival in 2024 were domestic violence, drug trafficking, and economic crime; urban violence found her anyway. [Source: communiqués via France 3/Le Populaire, ICI, JORF]

**The departmental president, Jean-Claude Leblois (Socialist).** On site, then in the radio studio: "A terrible vision... The mairie is the symbol of the Republic. Attacking the mairie is attacking that symbol... I hope the authors will be identified and condemned. Violence is not an act to be trivialized; it is the opposite of democratic expression. Public service will stand firm. We must form a union... barbarity has no place in our country." [Source: ICI]

**The Interior Minister, Laurent Nuñez.** 550,000 demonstrators, ~400 arrests, "the very great majority adults." "Not at all the 2018 Yellow Vests movement" — declared marches gave the rioters something to graft onto. "A very clear ultra-left handprint" — and, in the same breath, "opportunist delinquents" from mid-afternoon. "Extremely determined" groups, "very mobile," attacks "simultaneous." He ruled out a state of emergency and promised the authors would be found and made "criminally and civilly responsible." [Source: LCI/TF1, Le Monde]

**The Prime Minister, Sébastien Lecornu.** Crisis cell at the Interior Ministry at 8 p.m. with the justice and interior ministers; "organized ultra-left groups" as the day's authors; "delinquents mixed in to pillage and destroy"; no one "will impose their political agenda by violence"; the casseurs-payeurs bill "to the end" — "public money is scarce: it must fund our public services, not endlessly repair what rioters destroy." His government had tried to defuse the fuel grievance ten days earlier with the strategic-reserves release; it was not enough. [Source: X statements, franceinfo, Maire-info]

**The President, Emmanuel Macron.** On Saturday: "No place for violence in our Republic. Those who smashed, attacked prefectures as well as businesses, looted even our town halls, will be prosecuted and punished... We will all stand together against the sowers of chaos." On Sunday at 8 p.m., with the consolidated toll in hand, the outgoing president addressed the country at length. The authors of the violence, he said, were "agitators from ultra-left groups, joined by numerous delinquents." He expressed "shame." He asked the question that became the headline: "How can French people do this to France?" — and answered it with a program: prefects and mayors would be empowered to ban gatherings outright; those who destroyed "must pay," those who looted "must repay," those who "sought to bring down the Republic will answer for it"; the entire government would sit down with the young "to make concrete proposals" this week; and the country "needs order, calm, and not escalation," with every party and union expected to condemn "without ambiguity." He spoke of the young people's "deep anxiety" and warned against ever confusing them with the destroyers. The political class judged the speech instantly and almost universally: "empty and disconnected," said the left and parts of the right; the far right called it the address of a power "out of breath"; his own prime minister had already announced the coming week would be heavy. The president flew to the Gulf on Monday anyway. [Source: X, franceinfo, RFI, Le Monde, TF1]

**The political ecosystem.** Mélenchon (LFI) condemned the pillaging ("nothing to do with pillage and sacking") while denouncing an "erratic command" of the police — the government called this excuse-making. The Socialists condemned the "casseurs" while noting it seemed "easier to pillage than to march" in places. On Limoges' own council, the smaller left groups issued a joint condemnation — "popular anger can and must never be expressed through the destruction of our common good" — while the LFI-led opposition group did not co-sign it by cutoff, an absence that speaks its own language. The RN demanded immediate trials, benefit suspensions for repeat offenders, and the full casseurs-payeurs law; its local councilors' municipal bulletins speak of incivility, insecurity, and "restoring order" — coded, never ethnic. Ciotti claimed "Islamo-leftism." Days earlier, government spokesperson Bregeon had gone further than her ministers, naming two LFI parliamentarians — Louis Boyard and Bally Bagayoko — as people who "stoke this violence"; the Socialist first secretary Olivier Faure condemned the casseurs while asking the President to receive the students; and conservative presidential contender Bruno Retailleau, who has long pressed to dissolve LFI as a "seditious party," demanded "the implacable force of the State." Retail federations and business lobbies condemned the pillaging. The unions condemned attacks on their own marchers (Toulouse's CGT claimed police aggression on four union leaders; the prefect said 200 individuals attacked the union head-of-march).

The reactions kept coming through Sunday. The Greens' leader — gassed and kettled at République that same day — demanded the interior minister's resignation, "no outcome other than his resignation," an echo of the far right's identical demand of the day before; she also asked the minister to "calm his police." The CGT's general secretary said the president "has understood nothing of what is happening in this country." From the other flank, the conservative presidential candidate demanded a state of emergency "without delay," as in 2005, with immediate trials and parental responsibility — "what France has been living since Saturday is no longer a riot; it is the authority of the State being defied, everywhere, at the same time." The far right's leader pronounced the address "lamentable" and the power "out of breath"; a former Socialist president called the situation grave and the violent "impostors dressed as rioters" to be "implacably condemned." The police unions' register hardened — "we have killers in front of us," said a southern representative, "some colleagues came close to death" — while an LFI parliamentarian countered with the doctrine thesis: "the French public-order doctrine has changed; tear gas has become the baseline." Each of these is a claim about the day, recorded as a claim; the judging belongs to the courts and, eventually, the voters.

**The march.** On Sunday at 5 p.m., in a city that had watched its town hall burn the night before, several thousand people — the city hall's count said 7,000 — gathered at Place de la République and walked in silence to the ruined building. Nineteen metropolitan mayors with their councils, the presidents of the department and the region, the national association of mayors led by David Lisnard, and a government minister stood together under the slogan "Wounded Limoges, battered Limoges, but Limoges standing; the Republic will not yield." The association of mayors, calling for rallies in front of town halls across France the next day, chose its words with care: it condemned the violence "on the occasion of demonstrations which, for their part, respected public order" — the institutions, too, refusing to conflate the marchers with the fire. The mayor, at the head of the march, saluted "all the republican camps — with the exception of France Insoumise, largely responsible for this chaos." Those were his words, hours after that party's local deputies had themselves condemned the sacking in writing. A march of unity, with a line drawn through it: the city's politics, like the country's, was already choosing sides about what the fire meant.

**The church bells.** Two archbishops added their voices — Marseille's condemning the "cycle of this violence" in a city that had seen its own town hall's statue of Marianne — the helmeted female figure who personifies the French Republic — thrown to the floor; Lyon's calling the destructions "the sign of a society that is ill," whose impasse "engenders more injustices and more divisions." And in Montreal, thousands of kilometers away, a solidarity march for the French students denounced — in the mirror image of the American framing — police brutality. [Source: La Dépêche live, LCP, X statements, limoges.fr bulletins; BFM, Le Monde, Midi Libre; archdioceses; Le Devoir]

## PART 8 — The Information War: A Field Guide to What You Saw Online

Putting the machinery together, here is the complete anatomy of the false-narrative pipeline as it operated around this fire — the same pipeline that produced, and will keep producing, claims about who burned Limoges:

1. **Seed.** A political figure asserts a frame (identity-flavored) without evidence, or an old clip is pulled from the archive (July footage, 2022 footage).
2. **Amplify.** Foreign-run or engagement-farming accounts repost; misinformation earns roughly ten times the engagement of ordinary content, so the incentive is structural, not incidental. AI-generated posters and fake quotes fill the gaps between real clips. On October 18, this step reached its ceiling: the vice president of the United States posted the burning Limoges city hall itself — the most authentic clip in circulation — as the visual proof of an immigration claim no authority in France had made. When the amplification layer reaches that altitude, the seed no longer needs to be true; it needs only to be watchable.
3. **Contaminate.** Legitimate outlets' social-media teams, working at speed, recycle undated material (five documented cases in five days around Limoges alone — including one of this document's own early errors, corrected in the text).
4. **Detect.** Fact-checkers (France 24, AFP Factuel, TF1 VÉRIF) verify dates and origins — typically ten to twenty hours behind the seed, by which time the clip has crossed oceans.
5. **Persist.** Debunked claims never die; they wait for the next event. France has watched this with recycled riot footage, fabricated casualty claims, and "needle attack" panics. The Limoges fire will feed this cycle for years.

**Who covered it, and how that skews what you saw.** The media-bias aggregator Ground News tracked the Limoges story in real time: at various points on October 17, right-leaning outlets accounted for 54–60% of the story's coverage (five to thirteen sources), while other clusters of the same story showed as little as one or two sources, all left-coded. The lesson is not that one side owns the story; it is that different audiences received measurably different selections of it — and that outrage-friendly framing, whoever produces it, travels farthest. The most complete minute-by-minute record remained the local ones: ICI's live blog, France 3's on-scene team — journalist Fabrice Bidault filed the line that went around the world: "The Limoges city hall is burning. Unprecedented." — and the regional daily's running file. The international wires and broadcasters (AFP in English, Reuters, the BBC, DW, France 24) told the story almost uniformly as grievance-plus-hijack — a cost-of-living revolt partly captured by rioters. None adopted an ethnic framing. That is why the battleground for the identity claim was never the press; it was the unindexed layer beneath it.

And the counter-measures that exist, named: VIGINUM's monitoring of foreign interference (which caught fake "Muslim" websites impersonating communities in March 2026); the AFP Factuel naming of the fake-quote factory; platform AI-labels; the prosecutor's video exploitation; and — most old-fashioned but most reliable — the local newspaper's timeline. [Source: France 24, NewsGuard, Integrity Institute/Frontiers methodology, AFP Factuel, VIGINUM]

## PART 9 — What We Do Not Know: The Honest Ledger

A complete anatomy must include its own silences. As of this document's cutoff (the third day — October 19, 2026), the following remain unknown — some because the event is days old, some because the institutions have not yet spoken, and some because the question may only be answerable in a courtroom:

- **The identity, age, residence, and affiliations of the people who set the fire.** Nineteen people were in custody within two days — but for the pillaging and street violence, a file whose published profile (minors aged 14–25, most without criminal records) describes a different population from the masked group that forced the doors. For the arson itself: no named suspect, no announced identification, no published profile. Everything about the fire-setters beyond "masked" is inference — informed, patterned inference, but inference.
- **Whether the October actors connect to earlier nodes** — the July 2025 ambush group, the October 1–13 school protesters, the traveling ultra-left milieu — awaits video cross-matching (the city's new cameras now loom large).
- **The chant question.** Crowds chanted on the forecourt (documented); what they chanted is not transcribed in any located source. Two expert analyses of the day's violence — a sociologist's on a regional live blog, a second sociologist's on national radio — exist as audio, untranscribed; their full content remains open.
- **The overlap question.** Nineteen people are in custody for the pillaging and street violence, a population skewing young and clean-recorded. Whether anyone from that file, or from the July 2025 ambush arrests, appears in the town-hall arson file is exactly what the organized-crime division's investigation must establish — and has not said.
- **The money figures.** The mayor's promised precise damage assessment had not been published; no insurer's estimate was official; the circulating €110–120 million figure was unverified; the Senate's rapporteurs present their conclusions on October 21; the parliamentary commission's membership is unannounced; the prime minister's visit to Limoges was promised "within the week," unscheduled.
- **The written complaint.** The mayor announced it on camera; the signed document had not surfaced at cutoff. (The city did file a written complaint for the October 1 damage — three weeks earlier.)
- **The final structural assessment** of the building (the fire service's technical verdict on what survives), the insured value and carrier of the city's policy, and the rebuild cost.
- **The aftermath's open tails.** The march happened — 7,000 by the city hall's count, the mayors' association rallying town halls nationwide the next day — but independent counts, the speeches' full record, and the presence or absence of each political group remain thinly documented. The LFI deputies of the department condemned the sacking in writing; whether the mayor's exclusion of them from "the republican camps" hardens into an institutional split is a story still moving. The region's and department's funding commitments, and the insurance federation's first estimate, had not been published. And the prime minister's visit — promised "within the week," with ministers, calendar, and means to be fixed — had not yet happened; what the State ultimately puts on the table will measure the "rain of millions" demand against the direct-budget promise.
- **The Senate's casseurs-payeurs debate on October 27** — where the bill will be measured against a live catastrophe for the first time.
- **The trial record** of the July 2025 minors (heard October 15, 2025) — its outcome sits in the local press archives and may or may not have left a trace in the local mood.
- **The origin of the ethnic claim itself.** The claim's origin is no longer hidden — it is simply not evidentiary. It was spoken by an American vice president and a former American president, powered by the fire's own imagery, and carried into the local press of the city concerned. What remains unproduced, by every speaker and every amplifier, is the one thing that would make it true: a name, a face, a charge sheet. If you have encountered a version of this claim with more substance, the verification stack is ready: date the footage, source the account, check the tactics against the documented foreign-playbook (VIGINUM's "Blackcore" attribution), and weigh it against the standing official record — which, from the prefect to the president to the prosecutor to the international wires, says the same thing: no link established between immigration and these events.

## PART 10 — The People in This Story

Because every balance sheet and pattern list can hide the humans: a municipal employee wept in an elected official's arms while her workplace burned.
